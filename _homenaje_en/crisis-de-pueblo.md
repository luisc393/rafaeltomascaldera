## 1

“Never as at present has our country needed greater attention in examining its problems as a people, because never as now has the crisis of substantive values been so evident. Nor, since the heroic age, has our country ever faced a greater number of problems at once.”

So wrote Mario Briceño Iragorry, with a prophet’s voice, in *Mensaje sin destino* (Message Without Destination), brought out seventy-five years ago. That message has resonated strongly in the conscience of those who have read it over this time and, as we see, still calls us to think deeply about our problems.

There Don Mario argues, to put it briefly, that ours is a *crisis of the people*, a lack of cohesion with characteristic manifestations (the type of person cultivated in our milieu), which leads us again and again to what, borrowing the title of a book by Arturo Uslar Pietri, we may describe as the *making and unmaking of Venezuela*.

Briceño Iragorry points to ignorance of our history as the root of this lack of backbone. He was also able to say that we are “traditionally deniers of tradition,” with a need to change everything that some have called an “Adam complex”: as if each of us were the first man on earth. And, before me, there was chaos.

## 2

Since the *Mensaje* appeared in 1951, academic studies of Venezuelan history have multiplied. The problem, then, is not a lack of information or of relevant bibliography. It is an attitude that leads us to ignore what we have been and, consequently, to fail to recognize how we are.

“The history of Venezuela has been one of hardships,” writes Uslar Pietri. “Of hardships and of unease. Of great and boundless hopes and difficult readjustments to reality. Of placing in tomorrow and in the distance the object of passionate and dazzling action, for not wanting, or perhaps not being able, to keep to the present and to what is given.”

Have we not realized that the democratic Civil Republic which we lived through for forty years (with the imperfections of everything human) was, in our country, *a historical singularity*?

On the twenty-fifth anniversary of the 1961 constitution, in a joint session of the legislative chambers, Rafael Caldera tallied up our civilian governments before 1958: “In 115 years, from the consolidation of the Republic until 1945, civilian presidents held power for less than 8: José María Vargas for 13 months, Vice President Andrés Narvarte for 9, Manuel Felipe de Tovar for 13, Vice President Gual for less than 4, Rojas Paúl for 2 years and Andueza Palacio for 2 years.”

## 3

The question we must then explore is this: how was that democratic Civil Republic possible in Venezuela?

Without at least trying to take on the meaning of our history, with our own cultural situation, we will not be able to understand it, nor will we be capable of rebuilding it.

It was not, no, the fruit of transferring or copying foreign institutions. Not the implantation of a liberal democracy in the Anglo-Saxon manner, thanks to oil wealth and the presence of foreign companies. Our leaders knew Bolívar’s admonition at Angostura: “Here is the Code we should consult, and not that of Washington!”, warning us against what we may call the *colonial mentality*, which considers better, always better, what is done abroad, be it in Paris, New York or Havana.

We have had a democratic republic when (let us put it this way) our civilian *caudillos* submitted to the law. A law, a constitution, born of our historical experience and of sound training in the art of law.

What does that imply? We can mention three key aspects: the predominance of reason over passion; of justice over arbitrariness; of the pursuit of the common good, not of individual gain.

Reason opens itself to considering what is good according to truth, and reaches a universal discourse, valid for society as a whole.

Justice (the striving for justice) leads us to seek that each person have what is theirs, that is, what is due to them as a person and for their fulfillment.

The pursuit of the common good must be the intention of every ruler who takes on their mission with integrity, in order to foster the development of persons and institutions and to protect the nation from external threats.

## 4

When a change of constitution was proposed (did we not want to notice?), outside and beyond the “moribund” constitution of 1961, what we changed was the foundation on which the State rested. We passed, once again in our history, from the rule of law to the arbitrary will of a *caudillo*.

## The rupture was complete

The new emerging military group spoke, almost like Cipriano Castro a century earlier, of *new men, new ideals, new procedures*. Thus we seemed to have begun and ended the twentieth century at the same point.

The act of voting was taken as the measure of a true democratic life. But the Venezuelan’s attachment to the vote, real as it undoubtedly is, is perhaps also, more than civic virtue, a manifestation of that sense of individual importance that leads us to cross a street anywhere and at any moment, heedless of traffic signals or of the convenience of others. It guarantees neither freedom nor justice.

The new politicians took up the discourse of rupture without attending to the existential conditions that had made the democratic Civil Republic possible.

## 5

We were presumptuous enough to believe that everything was done. That the Civil Republic was firmly established. That the institutions would sustain social life in freedom.

But it is not a matter of procedures; it is a matter of virtue. Let us listen to Bolívar again: “virtuous men, patriotic men, enlightened men make up republics.”

We face the challenge of rebuilding the institutions that will once again make freedom possible. It falls to us to take up again, in difficult conditions in the life of the world, the path of our country’s development.
