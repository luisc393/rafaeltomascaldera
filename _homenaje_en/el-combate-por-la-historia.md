To awaken the soul, to move consciences towards the foundation and transcendent destiny of human existence, to serve as a leaven helping others to live fully, humanly and as Christians: these phrases sum up the spirit and the unity underlying the life and work of Rafael Tomás Caldera. Whether in intimate and fraternal conversation (to which he so generously devotes so much time), in the classroom as one of his students, or as readers of his texts, not a few of us have had our lives touched to the core by the spur of his wise and serene word.

He has brought the same commitment to his struggle for the spiritual health of our country. Convinced that what ultimately holds us together as a nation is a shared understanding of how we ought to live and of what human fulfillment consists in, his writings seek to make visible to citizens that truth of the good which nourishes the life of society.

His essay “Existencia e historia” (Existence and History), collected in *En busca de nuestra expresión* (In Search of Our Expression), is a magnificent example of his purpose of reminding society of the experiences and values that give it meaning. There Rafael Tomás Caldera states that perhaps nothing is more necessary in today’s Venezuela than a “struggle for history.” In his view, the very possibility of Venezuela finding its way again, at one of the most critical moments of its historical existence, depends on this task.

Caldera starts from an observation about our cultural reality as a nation. Among us there is a paradoxical combination of pessimism and dreamy illusion. We believe we have never been happy, but we trust that we soon will be, provided we find a radically new solution. Hence our recurrent messianism: the endless search for providential saviors who promise to start history from scratch. This attitude of rejecting the past perpetuates itself as a tradition (a negative tradition) that condemns the country, as in the myth of Sisyphus, to a cycle of repeated frustration and disappointment. Because of that *negative tradition*, “we arrive without having arrived,” as Mario Briceño Iragorry put it in the expression Caldera takes up.

## Timeless moments and the narrative structure of existence

The problem goes much deeper than simple ignorance of the facts of the past. To understand why history is indispensable, Caldera examines the question by taking it down to the very structure of our personal existence.

Every human action proceeds from deliberation and is directed towards ends that the person chooses. When we examine our lives, the intimate connection between actions scattered in time can only be illuminated from within and unified in the light of the final meaning that animates them. In this effort, the most radical question concerns what, amid the flow of actions and experiences, has lasting consistency.

Reflecting on the phrase “timeless moments” that T. S. Eliot evokes in the last movement of his *Four Quartets*, Caldera invites us to remember those instants in which, immersed in time, we somehow touch what does not pass away. In the experience of beauty, as also in the encounter with truth and goodness, there is an anticipation of fullness that does not belong to time and yet occurs within it. Those experiences reveal, in the midst of the temporal flow, a deeper meaning.

When the question of meaning is pursued to its ultimate consequences, the inner encounter with truth in these experiences allows us to recognize a light that does not change, an immutable truth not subject to the fluctuations of consciousness or the corruption of time. Temporality is then transformed: it ceases to be mere passing and becomes a “not yet,” a time of hope directed towards an end.

## From the person to the people: the substance of history

In an analogous way, this truth about personal existence also applies to the life of peoples. The substance of history, in Eric Voegelin’s formulation that Caldera adopts, consists in the experiences through which humanity gains an understanding of itself and of its limits. Timeless moments are not merely private events. Some of those crucial experiences take place within a community and come to shape its deepest identity.

The establishment of the republic as a way of life, the experience of peaceful coexistence within a just order, or the growing understanding of the fundamental equality of all persons are historical achievements through which a nation discovers something about itself and its destiny. What is decisive is to recognize, through lived experience, what has revealed something permanent about our humanity and our condition as a people. A society that keeps alive the memory of such experiences has a firm foundation on which to act.

## The hope born of tradition

Our crisis in Venezuela may lead us to disown the past and to think that nothing of what we have been deserves to be preserved, so that all that remains is to begin again. The “struggle for history” is directed against that temptation. It is a matter of recovering the memory of those concrete experiences in which our civic tradition has revealed its highest goods and been able to glimpse its destiny.

Those crucial experiences are first lived by particular people (a leader, a founder, an intellectual), but it is not enough for them to remain locked in individual memory. They must “resonate” in the collective consciousness through public discourse and education, through the symbols and institutions that make them present to each generation. Faithful historiography is therefore indispensable at the collective level since, unlike persons, society has no organic consciousness of its own.

Hope does not lie in starting again from scratch, nor in waiting for another providential savior, but in recognizing, in our own history, experiences, values and achievements that have not lost their force and that can once again guide our common life: the dignity of the person, the value of freedom, the demand for justice. That is where the true substance of our history lies.

This, in the end, is the struggle for history that Caldera proposes. To recover the truth of our history is to recognize, even in the midst of failure, that on which it is still possible to rebuild.
