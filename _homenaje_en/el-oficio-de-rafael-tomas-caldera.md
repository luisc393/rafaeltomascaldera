## A wise man for the twenty-first century

For years I have asked my students, at different universities and in various disciplines, how they imagine a wise person (intellectually, morally, spiritually and physically speaking). Give or take a few details, the same answer comes back. They picture an old man[^1] with a deep gaze, rather small and thin, silent, sparing in his advice but profound, and predominantly Eastern (Chinese or Indian). He does not work, because “all he does is think” while contemplating nature. They do not imagine him concerned with the public affairs of his country or teaching at a university. There is some truth in the students’ picture. Clearly the wise man is not a political activist, nor an economic *manager* or an ontological *coach*. What is proper to him is the “fruitfulness of contemplation”: ultimately, the contemplation of truth, and even more, the contemplation of the Truth,[^2] not in order to become absorbed in it, but in order to share it freely: “if people lack free time, talent or the will to work, society may sink into ignorance, error and falsehood, and the social process then feeds on sophistry or, in its contemporary version, on propaganda, whether commercial advertising or ideological indoctrination. What is needed, then, is the effort and the word of some who will once again make the truth manifest, taking on the grave calling of the wise man, since it is intrinsic to that calling ‘to communicate what has been contemplated’” (*El oficio del sabio*, p. 14).

## *Officium sapientis*

After this question and the analysis of their answers, the students must read Rafael Tomás Caldera’s essay *El oficio del sabio* (The Calling of the Wise Man).[^3] I do not know its “impact factor” in Venezuela,[^4] but in Chile, at least, it leaves no reader indifferent. In this sense it is a useful essay,[^5] not only because it corrects my students’ error and frees them from their ignorance, but also, and above all, because it often awakens in them a certain friendship with knowledge. They also come to understand the difference between *curiositas* (so deeply rooted in university culture thanks to digital platforms, social networks and AI) and *studiositas*. Taking as his example Thomas Aquinas, “the holiest of the learned and the most learned of the saints”,[^6] Rafael Tomás Caldera clearly,[^7] in an elegant style and with profound ideas, describes for us what is proper to wisdom. A wise man who does not turn his back on the world or disdain reality but, on the contrary, investigates, scrutinizes and judges it. The wise man, “placed then between society and the ultimate realities of the universe, has the task of communicating the truth he has meditated” (*El oficio del sabio*, p. 14).

## The need for wisdom

The search for wisdom is not an intellectual pastime, an exotic luxury, or a vocation reserved for a few university professors devoted to the humanities. It engages the whole person and all persons, because the search for truth is inscribed in the depths of our nature. In this sense, our author reminds us that wisdom “is not a matter of cultivating a hobby to fill the leisure hours that fortune grants us. It is a task, responsibly undertaken. In its classical sense, it is a *calling*, a service and a duty. It is born, then, of a radical commitment to truth, which seeks to carry the great questions about the world, man and God to their very depths; and it is carried out within a particular society, a culture and a time” (p. 14). In this context, universities have an unavoidable role. Unfortunately, at least in Chile (and I say this with some first-hand knowledge), many universities long ago ceased to be that community of teachers and students who disinterestedly seek the truth.[^8] Among its dire consequences we find the fragmentation and hyper-specialization of knowledge. Universities ought to foster a true *universitas*, because one of the essential features of wisdom is the search for truth, since man “does not wish to live in deception or in error. Man, who seeks his own fulfillment (...). That is why human life together must ultimately be founded on truth, and on the truth about man.” This task, to which we are naturally inclined, is not easy given the times we live in, in this “liquid society” (Bauman) or “burnout society” (Byung-Chul Han). “Bound by necessity, by the daily labor of sustaining life in all that it consumes and demands, people lack enough time for the slow task of seeking the truth, refining what they have found, passing it on and increasing it” (*El oficio del sabio*, p. 9).

## Wisdom and friendship

There is no genuine wisdom without friendship. Rafael Tomás Caldera is a philosopher, and therefore a friend of wisdom. He has woven a deep friendship with knowledge, with work well done, with his students and colleagues, and above all with God. His vast academic work and the formation of thousands of young people bear witness to this. That friendship is anchored in friendship with God, from whom all good comes, the source of all wisdom and the womb of our existence.

## Final remarks

I end these brief reflections with a memory. Before meeting Rafael Tomás in person, I had read part of his academic work thanks to a mutual friend who was living in Santiago at the time. Later I met him in Caracas, in 2010 if I remember correctly. He picked me up at the hotel and invited me to lunch at an Italian restaurant whose name I do not recall, though I do remember enjoying an exquisite *risotto* and a very pleasant conversation about shared themes and concerns. On that occasion a friendship began to be forged that neither the passage of time nor distance has swallowed up, for it is, I believe, interwoven with a communion of ideas and with my admiration for one who has made wisdom his calling.
