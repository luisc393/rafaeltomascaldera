The “llanero” harp arrived on a ship. The Jesuits who brought it taught it in the missions; then it left the cloister and became, without any decree, the voice of the savanna. Nothing in its origin heralded the joropo.

I mention this because in the book at hand, brilliant and for years impossible to find, a harp appears in the commentary on Gallegos’s story “Pataruco”: Pedro Carlos, a pianist trained in Europe, comes home wanting to *create* the national music, and fails; he concludes that he fails because he is incapable, and that he is incapable because he is of mixed race, and he gives up music. Years later he walks into a country hut, borrows a harp, plucks a few notes and discovers that he was nothing but a harpist.

Caldera reads the scene as the acceptance of one’s own being. Perhaps.

## The persistent question

The book starts from a question we ask ourselves persistently: what are we? Caldera’s first move is to be suspicious. It is not that Venezuela lacks an identity. If the country exists, how could it not have one? The question is badly framed, and it is worth less for what it asks than for what it suggests.

Then comes Caldera’s diagnosis. In Gallegos and in Briceño Iragorry he finds one illness with two faces: presumption and pessimism, the love of adventure and flight. We are capable of the great effort of a moment, not of the small effort of every day. But the cause is not laziness; it is a lack of hope.

Perhaps we distrust daily work not out of weakness of character, but because we have learned not to trust in tomorrow; and distrusting a tomorrow that has failed so many times is not irrational: it is reading the world correctly (see the *marshmallow* experiment). The diaspora suggests as much, sustaining in Madrid or Bogotá the daily effort it could not manage here. Character did not change: the ground did.

## Heritage and culture

Let us return to the small effort. It has a name, maintenance, and maintaining what one has received is not a condition of culture: it is culture. A city is never finished; while a lookout point is being inaugurated at one end, at the other a forty-year-old pipe is being replaced under the street. Likewise, no living body keeps its flesh or its blood: it endures by replenishing, and if identity were matter, no city would survive its repairs. Almost nothing remains of the Caracas of 1810, and it is still Caracas: what persists is not substance but care.

Caldera devotes an essay to the failure to do so: *Mentalidad colonial* (Colonial Mentality), thinking like a colony, “where one’s own life is a pale reflection of the true one, which takes place in the metropolis, real or imagined.” His example is architecture: Venturi could write *Learning from Las Vegas*, but here, with no Las Vegas, we copy all the same, and the black glass cube spends on air conditioning what it would not spend if it were made of brick; the opposite of the harp, it remains foreign to us because nobody adapted it to the sun here. The problem is not importing, but failing to translate.

Caldera says it better: the question is “how to express what is our own in the new language.” His authenticity is not a hidden depth to be discovered, but fidelity to what one wishes to express and determination to give it form.

## Essences

An essence answers *what is this?*, and it answers with a class: Pedro Carlos, a man; the harp, a chordophone. Understood this way, it admits no degrees (no Bengal tiger is less of a tiger than another), nor does it choose among anyone’s traits. But that is what is being asked of it. To say that Pedro Carlos is a harpist is not to name his class but to choose, among everything he has been (the one who left, the one who studied piano, the one who came back, the one who failed), the part that will stand for him, and to call the rest distortion. That is not an essence: it is an ideal, and one comes closer to ideals or further from them.

With justice there is no disguise: we ask about an ideal, and nobody expects a census in reply. It will be said that there are indeed degrees: someone who grew up abroad, someone who barely speaks of home. There are, but they are degrees of rootedness, not of belonging. The nationalist does not invent the degrees: he invents the depth of which they would be degrees, and from there decides who is surplus.

## Ceasing to ask

There are two ways to ruin a city. One is to build without repairing: the inaugurated project, and afterwards the water that never arrives; what Briceño Iragorry called having arrived without arriving. The other is to preserve without building, and Caldera saw this in Europe, whose “crystallized forms give consistency and produce rigidity.”

The same happens with peoples: a people is not something apart from those who make it up by living together. Whoever already knows what it is to be German, Spanish or English has, from that day on, something to defend against the newcomer, whose mere presence refutes the answer; the consequences were atrocious and are becoming so again. And there is an irony: a people that were a simple thing, with its own nature, would be something about which there was nothing to tell, since telling requires parts and an order among them. Certainty does not give peoples more identity: it leaves them less to say. Seen this way, our uncertainty is not only a lack: it is health.

Of his masters Caldera wrote that their journey had opened a space, begun a tradition. That is what must be said of him, and I say it with gratitude: arguing with him is possible thanks to what he has left us.

And he knew where philosophy is done: he warns in his epilogue that here reflection cannot absent itself from daily life without becoming mere diversion. Whoever refuses to accept the first answer is doing philosophy, inside or outside the university. And it is maintenance work: going back over what we took as answered, mending what use has worn, keeping it going one more day.

Let us reissue it. Not because it says what we are (it makes no such claim), but because it teaches us to distrust quick answers. A harpist is not a lineage: it is someone who plays. The same goes for peoples: the question is not answered once and for all; it is performed every day, until it rings true.
