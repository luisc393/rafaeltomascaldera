In March 2023 Rafael Tomás Caldera presented another book which, like all of his, is a work of deep reflection: *El poder y la justicia. Para jóvenes políticos* (Power and Justice: For Young Politicians).

## A logbook for the emerging generation

This work is, without a doubt, a kind of logbook in that sense. Its title, *El poder y la justicia*, announces two capital themes which, as history shows, have not always gone hand in hand, but have more often been at odds or far apart. And its suggestive subtitle, *Para jóvenes políticos*, signals its clear purpose of pedagogical and formative guidance for those who, still untainted, may feel, as Rafael Tomás rightly says, “called to politics.”

Thus, across the book’s eight chapters, the author sets out his reflections and clarifications “for those young people who feel called to devote their best efforts to building a better, more just and more fraternal society.” These discussions deal with the notions of power and justice, the understanding of politics, political formation, authentic human development, the human person and their dignity, the commitment to *exist with the people* (so dear to Jacques Maritain), and the concepts of the common good and freedom.

These are principles, the author says in his introduction, “that young politicians must make their own.” And he adds straightaway “that the program for forming those who may be fit for government should cover a wide range of subjects, enabling them to understand the time and the world in which they act. But there is no true education without that assimilation of principles which allows the discernment proper to a cultivated intelligence, one able to distinguish the degrees of certainty of what is asserted, and which strives to get to the heart of the matter.”

## A constant Christian inspiration

There is a constant Christian inspiration in every theme addressed (with special and repeated emphasis on the ethical meaning of politics), whether through various quotations from papal encyclicals of John XXIII, Paul VI, John Paul II and Benedict XVI, through major Church documents, or through the works of fundamental theologians such as Saint Thomas Aquinas and Saint Augustine, among others.

The theme of power and justice, the author notes at the outset, matters “because those terms can designate the basic coordinates of political activity and, therefore, of the vocation to engage in it.” And while it is true that the exercise of power is an essential part of political activity, power is *a principle of action* and not an end in itself, he explains, citing Saint Thomas Aquinas. Power, then, must be an instrument for ordering social life, which can only be done in the service of justice, defined as “the perpetual and constant will to give each one what is due to them.” Power must also be an instrument for the common good, based on ethical criteria and sustained by legitimacy both of origin and of performance.

For the author, the dilemma between power and justice is fundamental to the practice of politics, but that practice must in turn be guided by principles that are not mere abstract convictions but real purposes: among them, the dignity of the human person, the principle of subsidiarity in government action, social justice as guide and as action, the principle of the common good, the rights to economic initiative and to work, not forgetting the universal destination of the goods of the earth to meet the needs of all in solidarity.

## The ethical meaning of politics

There is an essential matter to which Rafael Tomás Caldera returns again and again, with good reason: *the ethical meaning of politics*, which, he explains, “sustained by the spiritual dimension as lived, leads to the rejection of pragmatism, that logic of numbers which can lead astray those who, with good will, take up the democratic struggle because, they tell themselves, ‘we have to win the elections.’ Sometimes, among those who seem interested in taking part in political struggle, one finds an attitude of disdain towards ideas. The point, they always say, is to be practical, not theoretical. They forget that human freedom, which ultimately drives the social process, is grounded in reason, and that reason feeds on truth.” And in distinguishing that pragmatism from practical sense, he warns that the former is “a substitution of ends, camouflaged under the appearance of practicality.”

Politics, in the end (and this is largely where its ethical foundation lies), has the common good as its purpose, “which,” the author adds, “demands the realization of justice. Without justice (the sentence is Saint Augustine’s), what are States but great robberies?”

Having built this general framework, Rafael Tomás Caldera then goes into what he calls the understanding of politics, broadly and deeply, as well as the vast and demanding field of the formation that politics requires. For politics must certainly be understood: one must know its scope and be aware of how much its ethical exercise can mean, above all if one comes to the noble and demanding office of governing for justice and the common good.

But it equally requires an effort of formation, of constant study and intellectual training. “Politics,” the author warns us, “as a deep calling experienced by the person, has nothing to do with the desire to be seen, to shine before public opinion; it is not that itch to talk about public affairs that gives rise to so much gossip and feeds so much slander; still less is it that ‘nightmare of grandeur’ that some cultivate in their souls and that leads them to live revolving around themselves. It is rather a call that we could not set aside without ceasing to be faithful to ourselves.”
