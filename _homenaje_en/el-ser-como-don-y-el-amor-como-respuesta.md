The rediscovery of the act of being and of the doctrine of participation in Saint Thomas Aquinas is one of the great philosophical events of the twentieth century. It showed that Thomistic metaphysics is not an ontology of abstract essences but a philosophy of being as act, the *esse*, received and participated at every instant from the source of all being, from that being which is at once truth, goodness and love.

Over more than six decades, the work of Rafael Tomás Caldera has shown that this existential metaphysics of participation leads to a deeper understanding of the human person, culminating in a metaphysics of the gift of self as the vocation and meaning of human life. The thesis that shapes this journey can be put as follows: because the *esse* of each creature is received as the gift of an original love, the fitting response to that gift can only be an equally loving act, the gift of self as the proper form of personal existence. Caldera thus helps us see that the wonder awakened by the question about the being of things leads us to seek the response that corresponds to the love that willed them.

## The act of being

Caldera’s philosophical journey rests on a distinction that Thomas Aquinas placed at the center of his metaphysics: the distinction between the essence of a thing (what it is) and its *esse*, the fact that it is. This received *esse* presupposes a source that does not in turn receive its own from another, because it is, simply, being itself. Saint Thomas named it *ipsum esse subsistens*, the subsistent act of being itself, “that which is by itself and wholly”, and which for that very reason “is the cause of everything that is by participation” (*Nostalgia y trascendencia. En busca de Aquel que Es*, p. 83).

This participation is not a property that an already constituted subject acquires afterwards: “participation in being cannot be conceived after the manner of a property that an already constituted subject acquires [...]. Participation in being is at the beginning and is constant: it is the presence of being at the heart of the entity” (p. 83). There is no core more hidden in the created entity than that received *esse*, which Saint Thomas calls “what is innermost in each thing” and “most deeply inherent in all things” (*S. Th.*, I, 8, 1, cited on p. 83).

## Reduction to love

The *esse* of each creature is received as a gift from an *esse subsistens* that is goodness and love without restriction. The communication of *esse* cannot be explained by any lack, for God needs nothing of what He creates: He gives being because He wills that things should be. At the origin of each thing, then, there is an act of love that does not follow upon its being but grounds it. Things do not exist first and are loved afterwards; they are loved, and that is why they exist. To create is thus an act of free love, not an impersonal or necessary emanation. To contemplate a thing in its being is to meet the echo of the love that gave rise to it. The foundation of the whole universe, Caldera explains, taking up Carlos Cardona’s expression, is a *reductio ad amorem*: “everything is reduced to love, to pure love, infinitely loving and liberal” (*Reducción al amor*, p. 38).

## Fullness and the gift of self

On this basis rests a decisive question that Caldera places at the center of his reflection. If love is the foundation of the being we receive as a gift, what response is fitting? The person is the only entity whose proper mode of being is self-possession and self-giving: in the person, *esse* is not received passively but actively exercised as freedom and love. Because of its very gratuitousness, that gift is moreover a *datio irredibilis*: a gift whose magnitude makes any equivalent repayment impossible, and which can only be answered by an equally gratuitous and free movement (*Sobre la naturaleza del amor*, p. 101). The response, then, is not payment but *redamatio* (pp. 62-63), the mutual indwelling of reciprocal love, to which we find ourselves called by a longing we discover inscribed in our being, as Saint Augustine teaches: “You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you” (*Confessions*, I, 1, 1).

This philosophical truth resonates in the formula of the pastoral constitution *Gaudium et spes*, which proclaims in the order of faith what the metaphysics of *esse* makes it possible to ground by way of reason. Caldera’s philosophy explores that central formula: “…man, who is the only creature on earth which God willed for itself, cannot fully find himself except through a sincere gift of himself.”

Far from being a paradoxical pious exhortation, this truth about human fulfillment, Caldera teaches us, is inscribed in the original love that constitutes our being (cf. “El don de sí”, in *Una civilización del amor*, pp. 59-77). Only because we have been loved first can we, in our turn, love and give ourselves. Thus we discover that the vocation and meaning of human life, as Saint John Paul II puts it in a phrase Caldera takes up, is “a gift that is fulfilled in being given”, and that the path to fullness is “to learn to love as we have been loved.”

Philosophy, Caldera warns, is not a saving knowledge. Nevertheless, meditating on foundations “can at least remove obstacles from the person’s path and, in some way, awaken the search…” (*Misterio de lo real / Vocación al amor*, p. 56). To guide us in that search, opening us to the love inscribed in the mystery of being and bringing us closer to Him Who Is, has been the purpose of a life devoted to giving others the fruit of his contemplation, *contemplata aliis tradere* (*El oficio del sabio*, p. 15). One of the most fruitful features of his thought is to show that the question of being and the question of love are, at bottom, one and the same: the most fundamental question we are called to answer on our way towards the full good.
