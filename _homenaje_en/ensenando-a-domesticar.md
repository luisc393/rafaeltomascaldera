The open existence is the happy achievement reached in the process that leads to human maturity. And are we to seek to understand it by reading a “children’s story” (*The Little Prince*)? Yes, because, as the author reveals in these pages (rediscovering the truth of the Gospel, Mt 18:3), that journey is a return to oneself, to the child we have always been; or, in the beautiful quotation from R. Tagore that he includes, “God waits for man to regain his childhood in wisdom.”

In *La existencia abierta* (The Open Existence) Dr. Caldera offers an interpretation for reading *The Little Prince*. He warns, however, that Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s work admits as many meanings as there are personal experiences among its millions of readers. Not a few of those readings, moreover, come from academic courses that use it as a textbook. Even so, Dr. Caldera’s pages stand out as a lofty summit of philosophical anthropology, and are so faithful to the original work that it seems as if the characters themselves (the Little Prince, the Aviator, the fox, the rose…) come out to meet us and explain the reason and the scope of their existence in the original.

It would be a beautiful and fruitful task to trace the parallels between the two works. For personal reasons that I explain below, and by way of a parenthesis, I will only point out that the very opening, the preface, offers a rich example. If the original is dedicated *to Léon Werth when he was a little boy*, Dr. Caldera dedicates his book to his many *young friends*, still children but eager to stop being so, whom he has sought to introduce to philosophy. With philosophy came the discovery of vital horizons, unsuspected until then. More often than not, it was the origin of a vocation, of that call to commit one’s life in an authentic, singular and definitive way.

I went through that. I lived it intensely. In my case it was pages from *Don Quixote*, the story of Grisóstomo and Marcela, and the thesis, framed as a question, of “whether there is an obligation to love the one who loves us.” A naive first attempt to answer yes, that it seems just and good; but then, how many considerations about that young man who gives everything, renounces everything, even his life, to win the beautiful shepherdess, who nevertheless does not want to stake her freedom on it! And then there were those partial essays, promptly reviewed, those brief conversations after class, and the more leisurely ones during office hours. And those questions that opened paths for thought but would no longer leave the heart in peace, because they pointed to something that went (far) beyond a “class assignment.” A slow and laborious effort to discern the different loves and the different responses they call for, and to begin discovering that there is only one love that binds in such a way that not only earthly life but eternal life is spent in returning it. How fortunate we have all been, all those *young friends* of Dr. Caldera, because, in the memorable phrase of Dr. Santiago Schnell, distinguished provost of Dartmouth College (United States), “he saved our souls”!

In the few pages of *La existencia abierta* it gradually becomes clear why the original story is an *essential* work “that can speak to everyone”: from the little ones who are amused by the charming drawings, to adolescents setting out to discover the world in search of friends with whom to share the adventure, to adults immersed in the cares of work and life (becoming *serious people*), to parents raising children full of unanswered questions, and even to those who, nearing the end of the day, cannot help wondering about the beyond. And among them, in a very special way, those committed to a married love that seeks to be definitive. How many marriages might be saved by keeping company with the Little Prince and the rose! And how many lonely hearts, shut away in social networks and shielded behind screens and headphones, might be redeemed by accompanying the *solitary* Aviator! But all of them need to have revealed to them those interpretive keys that can go unnoticed in a naive reading, and that perhaps the author himself did not intend. That is why *La existencia abierta* becomes as *essential* as the original story.

Mystery, the real, the nature of human encounter, of time, of beauty, friendship and spousal love, language, the problem of evil, guilt and sin, the passage from solitude to society, the contrast between person and persona, (un)happiness and pleasure, fidelity and sacrifice: these are themes that a masterly narrative moves through fluidly and quickly. But its reading should not be so; it calls for long pauses, calm reflection, at times even inner tears and (why not?) outward ones too, when one recognizes oneself in what is being signified. Just as the Little Prince wept before the roses, disappointed by what he took to be the small worth of his own person… and of his rose. For here comes the *crisis*, the end of the outward journey, and the return begins.

*La existencia abierta* shows us that this return, which we mentioned at the beginning, begins to happen when someone (like the fox) makes us realize what was already there, within us, but was not understood or could not be put into words. It is the moment for the marvel of illumination, by which we draw near to *understanding* love and to *seeing* with the heart; and then understanding and love are threaded together in an ascending spiral. The soul makes itself ready for eternity, and the body… for the snake.

There remains so much to say (one need only note the roll of masters who appear in these brief pages) that the invitation is worth making: to imitate the Little Prince in his repeated contemplation of sunsets, and to read and reread *La existencia abierta* with the same nostalgia (the *original love* that longs to return to the infinite).
