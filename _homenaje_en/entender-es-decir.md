Rafael Tomás Caldera has been a teacher to many Venezuelans. His main doctrines have not been written down. Perhaps what he says in this work holds true in his case: “the greatest teachers do not write” (*Entender es decir*, p. 32). Moreover, his work is a “struggle to recover what has been lost, and found and lost again and again” (p. 3). He does not try to establish a new body of doctrine, but to rescue the classical treasures buried in our annals. *Entender es decir* seeks to show how the work of Saint Thomas answers the problems connected with the relation between reflective consciousness in the manner of the Cartesian *cogito* and the original springs of consciousness itself (pp. 37 and 41).

## The core of the work

What we can control of our mental life through reflection is a tiny part of it. Life sails like a boat over the depths of the sea, driven as the wind blows into its spread sails. For example, the current of our imagination (which we do not control) leads us from not deliberating to deliberating, and the soundness of the deliberation that precedes our most momentous decisions depends to a great extent on fortune, and also on God, on the light and grace that come from above (cf. *Eudemian Ethics*).

The opening study, “Entender es decir”, explores the root of this structure of our being and acting. We know the things that present themselves to our senses, and they act on our soul. But we cannot understand them without a long prior sensory experience, which needs language to form the necessary imaginative schemes. Then the agent intellect intervenes, and our possible intellect *receives* the form of the thing known and “says” the *inner word* or concept, which is the act in which our understanding is consummated. Because of his condition as a rational animal, man cannot understand without “saying”, unlike the angels. But there is a further step: we say that concept to ourselves in a particular language. “*Water*,” said Helen Keller, for example. This step is “subsequent” in the genetic sense (p. 31), though not in time, because, although the word precedes this moment in the language, the soul of the word is the concept. The act of naming is the passage from the spiritual unconscious to consciousness.

Both the reception of forms and the formation of the inner word and of first principles happen mainly when we are not fully conscious. Yet from the first intellectual apprehension of [sensible] being (“everything has a name”, everything is *hoc aliquid*), which moves the child to seek actively the names of things, there occurs “his awakening to knowledge and, consequently, [there begins] the state of wakefulness […]” (p. 45). Even so, we are not able to reflect thematically on these decisive events until many years later, so that our reflective consciousness floats on an ocean of meaning that often remains implicit.

Two observations seem necessary. (1) The reception of the form and the saying of the concept cannot be separated in time (although they are two metaphysical “moments”, distinguishable by reason), because the reception consists in that inner saying. The concept is not a “natural sign” but the very act of understanding, a mere first act when we stop considering it. “I am the thing [I understand it] when I say it” (p. 31). And (2) I think, with Eric Voegelin, that it would be better to call this dimension not “unconscious” or “preconscious” but rather supra-rational. The way we know in simple apprehension and in the formation of first principles is a participation in the angelic way of knowing or in uncreated truth (cf. *S. Th.* I, q. 79, a. 4; *De veritate* q. 10, a. 6), even though it is connatural to us.

## Consequences

*Entender es decir* contains three essays. The most essential theme running through all three is that reflective consciousness floats both on an animal magma whose depth escapes our gaze and on an implicit ocean of meaning; that it is drawn from above by lights and strengthenings that come from God [and from the angels]; and that it is pushed by fortune in often wholly unforeseeable directions. It is therefore absurd: (1) to try to reduce our theoretical reflection to those principles that our reflective consciousness can establish. Recall Descartes’s bewilderment and wounded pride when he was reminded that the *cogito* presupposes notions and principles far prior to it (letter *To Clerselier*, June-July 1646). (2) To try to control our decisions as if they were “the conclusion of a kind of syllogism in the frontal area of reason” (p. 54). Our deep tendencies, the recognition of our own *pondus* of love, the truth of the good that presents itself to us, the recollection of the invisible norm, the realization of our creatureliness and of God’s leading role: all this breaks any attempt to reduce praxis to an algorithm. (3) To forget that the works of the poet and the inventor come partly from the preconscious (p. 62) and from divine [or angelic] inspiration (p. 61). (4) To try to reduce our identity to *memoirs* that *fabricate a character* (p. 67). It is very hard to find the plot line of our life. Not even the subject’s intention provides that thread, because it changes; because, when we reach what we intended, it proves insufficient; because it is subject to the turns of fortune. Moreover, in its frustration, which threatens to tear meaning away from us, *supra-meaning* appears (pp. 72, 74 and 75). When partial hopes are frustrated, basic hope emerges (Pieper, *Hoffnung und Geschichte*) and we realize that we are someone before God. The fundamental call to truth and the concrete divine vocation are the keys to our identity. The natural intention of happiness turns out to be nostalgia for the lost home, which is in God, and which, according to Boethius, we remember with a “blind memory” (pp. 70-71 and 74-78).

## Coda

This work takes a position that is exactly faithful to Saint Thomas amid the various neo-Thomist schools. John O’Callaghan, fascinated by the fact that the concept is an act of understanding the essence of the (sensible) thing and not a sign, denied that there were concepts (*Thomist Realism and the Linguistic Turn*) and reduced intellection to receiving the form. Francisco Canals, for his part, fascinated by the fact that understanding is not movement but act of the act, denied that concepts or principles originated in any influence of the form of sensible things on the intellect (*Tomás de Aquino, un pensamiento siempre actual y renovador*, 2019, pp. 89-95).
