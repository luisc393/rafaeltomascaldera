This short piece has its roots in Rafael Tomás Caldera’s essay *La filosofía en la nueva era tecnológica* (Philosophy in the New Technological Era). Its central argument is that humanity stands at the beginning of a new historical period in which the primary experience of the real is progressively replaced by a “second reality”, mediated by networks, screens and algorithms. The transformation of university education must be understood in light of this anthropological and cultural mutation.

The digital revolution we are going through is not simply a “fourth Industrial Revolution” but a civilizational revolution of a scale comparable to the Industrial Revolution or the transition to the Neolithic. Except that, instead of taking centuries or millennia, it is happening in decades.

## Starting points

We start from three points.

First: the main motive of most university students today is to obtain degrees in order to compete for better jobs. In the countries of the global North, around 50% of the population in the corresponding age brackets attend university.

Second: in the long run, though not a very long one, there will be few things that people do better than machines. More and more productive functions are performed better by AI-powered machines: faster, more cheaply and taking more data into account. Some functions will remain for humans, but probably not enough to generate billions of jobs.

Third: two functions coexist in university teaching: training for employability and the formation of the person. A good university uses the student’s motivation regarding employability to give them, as well, a deeper formation that serves them not only for employment but in every aspect of their lives.

From these starting points, we will consider three chronological futures for the university, which will presumably overlap within a few decades.

## Phase 1: increasing competition

The progressive adoption of AI-powered machines by companies will produce a net reduction in the number of professional jobs available relative to the working population. That means sharper competition for jobs, which will especially affect current and future university cohorts.

Universities already compete with one another through *rankings*, and their students, once admitted, try to reduce the cost of their grades in terms of effort by using ever-improving *online* AI tools.

In this phase, universities face the problem of keeping grades tied to the reflective learning of their subjects. Those of us who teach are seeing a massive shift in student learning towards skills in handling artificial intelligence that secure good marks.

But the problem is not only the use of AI to get better grades. As Caldera suggests, growing immersion in a second reality can weaken attention, reflection and immediate contact with the real. The university will have to defend precisely those human capacities that no technology can replace: contemplation, prudential judgment, friendship, dialogue and the formation of character.

## Phase 3: the point of arrival

In the long run, machines will win the competition for productive functions. What was becoming progressively harder for students will become generally impossible.

As early as 1930, John Maynard Keynes predicted that the problem of his grandchildren’s generation would be how to use their freedom from economic necessity to live wisely, agreeably and well.

He was not thinking of general impoverishment but of great political transformations. For consumer capitalism to work, people must have money to buy. If employment ceases to be the main source of income, societies will have to decouple income from employment, perhaps through a universal basic income, perhaps through broad participation in productive property, perhaps by other means.

In this phase, with employability gone as a general aspiration, the university’s main function will be the formation of the person. Here our argument converges with that of Rafael Tomás Caldera. The new technological era obliges us to rediscover what in the human being cannot be delegated to machines: inner life, the search for meaning, the capacity to contemplate truth, love, and the responsibility of building community.

If the problem of production passes progressively into the hands of artificial intelligence, the great human challenge will be to learn once again how to live wisely. The university of the future will be, above all, a school of humanity.

## Phase 2: doing politics

Between phase 1 and phase 3 there will be an intermediate phase of political reorganization. Redefining people’s sources of income, like any radical change in the regime of property, will necessarily be political.

New institutional arrangements capable of harnessing the productive capacity of digital technologies while maintaining social stability will emerge from great conflicts and negotiations.

The university will have to prepare its students to take part in shaping those institutions. The humanist university will be recognized by the fact that it teaches and motivates its students to engage in politics for the common good: not only their own good and that of people like them, but also that of those who have fewer resources and worse starting positions.

But politics itself requires people able to distinguish between reality and its representations, between truth and manipulation, between community and the mere aggregation of digitally connected individuals. Recovering the experience of the real and of inner life thus becomes a condition of possibility for democratic citizenship.

## Overlap

Our students in their twenties will probably encounter, over the course of their lives, all three phases we have described. That is why today’s university must, at the same time, increase their competitiveness through real learning; prepare them for the politics of a world in which employability will no longer be a general option; and cultivate those dimensions of human existence that will remain impossible to delegate even when the economic question has been solved.

Because, in the end, the decisive question will not be who produces best, but who is capable of living humanly in a world increasingly populated by intelligent machines.
