I barely knew any Spanish when I read a small book in one sitting while looking after the house of a Venezuelan friend living abroad in Chile. The author was Rafael Tomás Caldera, and his little book, entitled *La existencia abierta: para lectores de El Principito* (The Open Existence: For Readers of *The Little Prince*), offered a brilliant introduction to philosophy.

Only the great masters know how to write with a simplicity that, instead of concealing, illuminates the complexity of their subject. And only those who believe deeply in a true myth (C. S. Lewis), such as that of the Gospels, are aware that explaining a story well is a challenge proper to the philosopher. Certainly master Caldera (I count myself among his readers without having had the pleasure of meeting him in person) not only interpreted *The Little Prince* brilliantly, but also opened a new horizon for understanding the mystery of reality and of existence. That new gaze, contemplative and capable of wonder before being, prepared me for the true encounter that is needed in order to become a child and leave behind the foolishness, even the metaphysical foolishness, of adults.

It is impossible to keep a transformative experience to oneself, so that very night I plucked up my courage and began translating the book into my native language, Hungarian. The book sold well, and it fills me with gratitude that, that Christmas, some readers of *The Little Prince* received a beautiful gift from a Venezuelan thinker deeply convinced that culture is born of well-contemplated gestures of intellectual love.

## Reflections on love

For a discipline such as “philosophy”, whose very name contains the word “love”, clarifying that concept is its first and last task, and its *modus operandi* if it wishes to be faithful to its own vocation. And yet contemporary philosophy, which renders an important service to all the other disciplines that find themselves (knowingly or not) in deep crisis and are therefore fragmenting, does not dare to face that challenge. Does one see courses on the philosophy of love in university curricula? Certainly there is more than one course, and there are scientific books that offer extraordinary explanations and theories about certain aspects of the phenomenon, and do so very well; Caldera’s great treatise *Sobre la naturaleza del amor* (On the Nature of Love) offers a profound and systematic analysis of the first and greatest gift, showing the forgotten horizon of philosophy as true wisdom.

The starting point can be none other than the writings of Saint Thomas himself, deeply read. Not a textbook Saint Thomas, nor an Aquinas misinterpreted for alien ends, but a Thomas who articulates a vision worked out with the good sense of the new Adam, transformed by the infinite love of God.

Caldera does not claim to know more or better than his saintly guide, but he seeks to bring to light everything the tradition offers, in serious dialogue with contemporary voices.

## The path to full love

Translating the book on love into Hungarian, as a continuation of my intellectual adventure with Caldera, was, if not a political act, at least a small service to an intellectual community that, during decades of communist rule, lost the lively and wide-ranging discussion of sources as indispensable to a life of faith as the Fathers of the Church, Saint Thomas, or the much-loved Saint John of the Cross. There is no political revolution more radical than the one that springs from a renewed understanding of the love between God and man, for it is from this that community is reborn.

Although translating always entails a certain misunderstanding of the original, I do not think I am mistaken in grasping that Caldera’s purpose is eminently pedagogical: to teach how to love well. If the drama of human love is inevitable, the destructive misunderstanding of love as a dualism is not. Philosophical reflection on the human being’s capacity (*imago Dei*) to love in two radically different ways, when accompanied by a patient rumination on the texts of Saint Thomas, succeeds in elucidating what the “proper *ratio amoris*” is.

Discovering that love is a personal act of the human being, through which one perfects oneself by the very object to which it is directed, helps us better understand the paradox of human love, which is naturally directed towards a supernatural object.

Caldera opens up the human heart: he articulates the experience of the sincere person who recognizes their capacity for selfishness, “a love of need”, and, at the same time, the possibility of an authentic love, “a love of self-giving”. The Venezuelan professor shows that acting *ex amore sui* does not contradict a higher act of love, perfected by grace, which may be called *ex amore Dei*. His masterly analysis of love as *virtus unitiva* preserves both the distinction and the unity between reason and faith, the natural and the supernatural, eros and agape.

Contemporary culture and the new generation, which are our immediate responsibility as intellectuals, certainly know that the human being is made to love. What is new, and so suggestively illustrated in Caldera’s treatise, is the possibility of loving what is highest and most perfect as an existential possibility. The novelty that never loses its freshness is to love the absolutely good, that is, to love God above all things, which reorients a person’s will to such a degree that they become capable of loving the absolute in the smallest things, even in their own fragile person.

## The philosopher’s calling as promoter of a culture of love

Rafael Tomás Caldera exercises the great calling of the philosopher by illuminating love not as one phenomenon among many requiring philosophical reflection, but as the phenomenon to which everything else is reduced, because it is the origin and end of all things. His great contribution to a society in imminent danger of falling into narcissism is to teach us, through argument and in systematic fashion, that authentic *amor sui* exists only when love reaches its fullness as love of God for His own sake.
