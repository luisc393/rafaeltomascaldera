This conversation with Rafael Tomás Caldera took place in the middle of the year, in Tinajero. Many of the events we have since lived through had not yet happened. Even so, in the sharing of words and ideas, and with remarkable lucidity, his answers glimpse what was coming, describe paths already travelled and still to be travelled, and leave a trail for those who wish to go deeper into the common project that we, as a country, must recover.

As a journalist I had never spoken with him before. What surprises is the familiarity that arises at once: the closeness of someone used to guiding and teaching, a teacher in the classroom. In this approach to the Catholic philosopher and intellectual there is no room for clichés, nor for family comparisons of any kind. Rafael Tomás Caldera is who he is. No labels. A thinker who has devoted his life to cultivating the intellect and to the search for truth, and who this year, in recognition of his career, received an honorary doctorate from Universidad Monteávila and the 2026 Democratic Values Award in the category “Freedom of Thought”, granted by the Center for Political and Government Studies of Universidad Católica Andrés Bello. On that occasion he warned that Venezuela must soon return to democratic normality; without it there will be no peace, and we will not be able to take up again the path of our people’s development.

**Venezuela has suffered devastation in every respect. It has lost a third of its population, above all young people; perhaps a higher percentage if we speak of the working population. And we live at a time when many talk about redefining what we Venezuelans are as a people, as a nation, and many different theories arise. What are we Venezuelans as a human community? How would you define us?**

It is the same with that question as with the analysis of time. The present appears as a point without extension between past and future. So, to see what we are as a people, we must look at what we have been and what we want to be.

As for what we have been, the heroic history of Venezuela weighs on all of us, even if vaguely. It makes us Venezuelans feel somewhat exceptional. In any case, it is double-edged. We achieved Independence; we even helped bring independence to other countries. But the other edge of the sword is that it was a rupture.

A great rupture with the colonial period, one that has gone on perpetuating itself. Our Independence came after the French Revolution, which was radically rationalist and a revolution of rupture. And we inherited that. It is part of what we have been. We were left with the inheritance of always wanting to do something new. That gives content to Mario Briceño Iragorry’s idea that we are deniers of tradition.

When we ask ourselves what we want to be, that mortgage leads us to dream of something great but not very well defined. And since we do not have the model in our own roots, we look for it abroad. Hence, in a way, we are always copying in order to modernize the country. Modernizations that, being a transfer of procedures, styles and external things, end up breaking a little more with tradition and creating disorder in life.

A lack of harmony between what we have been and what we want to be. It translates into thinking that we can achieve things easily, by improvising. Briceño Iragorry described it in an essay called *Pequeño tratado de la presunción* (A Short Treatise on Presumption). He uses a formula: “arriving without having arrived”. The person who gains a university degree or a position without truly being prepared for it. But presumption has pessimism as its counterpart. It seemed easy to reach; we have not reached it. Here we are always failing. Until the cycle repeats itself, with the proposal to make everything new again: new men, new ideals, new procedures. And so, again and again, we attempt order in social and political life without fully achieving it.

That gives rise to, or allows, governments of force. The elementary fact of politics is that someone gives an order and it is carried out. That someone has the right to give the order and another must obey it: that is the problem of justification, of legitimacy. When that does not exist, the most immediate thing is force. A society cannot live without government, and a government cannot live unless its orders are obeyed. If there is no substratum in the civil tradition that has made it possible to arrive at a democratic republic, we will have governments of force, repeated again and again, adorned, to be sure, with what is borrowed from the heroic tradition.

That is a first answer. The second answer to what we are is what we want to be and, better still, what it falls to us to be and to do. What does that mean? Let us take an example. Our oil wealth has international importance, but it has to be ours. If we do not take control of our oil, others will take control of us through oil. We will not be independent.

Now it falls to us, very importantly, to rebuild the country’s institutions, its social life, its physical infrastructure. As was our project, partly achieved, we need democracy and development. For all of this we must, above all, recover the common project. Not a situation in which one group wants to impose itself and dominate, but a society that aspires to a better life, built together.

**But, as we were saying, Venezuela has suffered devastation in every respect…**

I have quoted several times a phrase of Briceño Iragorry in *Mensaje sin destino* (Message without a Destination) where he speaks of a *crisis of the people*. He said it for several reasons. One of them, a very important one, is the disarticulation of Venezuelan society. That became very serious from Chávez onwards, because Chávez cultivated it.

He cultivated confrontation from the first day. From his first speech as president-elect he divided Venezuelans between those who were with him and the others. And he would govern only for those who were with him. An error of political philosophy, since a ruler is the ruler of everyone. Chávez sowed discord in people’s hearts, for reasons that were no reasons at all.

**Were they ideological reasons?**

I don’t think so. One of the things that undoubtedly marks us Venezuelans, if you will forgive me for putting it this way, is that we are somewhat impulsive. We let ourselves be carried away by what sounds good, by what excites us at a given moment. Things were mixed together that were not properly ideological. The commander went from a scheme of caudillo, army and people (inspired by Ceresole) to talking later about twenty-first-century socialism, when he fell under the tutelage of Fidel, whom he called his spiritual father. What happened here was a lust for power and wealth.

**You have told me that Chávez caused that disarticulation of Venezuelan society, and that he sowed that discord above all in the hearts of Venezuelans. Speaking of traditions, I would ask you: what happened to that community?**

This disarticulation of the Venezuelan, who denies his tradition or wants a new country without taking into account what came before, was programmatic in Chávez’s case. He embodied to the utmost the idea that he was the new. We saw it when he repudiated the “Fourth Republic”, a very ambiguous expression, because it covered everything from Páez to him; that is, the whole life of independent Venezuela. Something out of any real context.

The country’s institutions were dismantled one after another. One of his hallmarks was to subvert hierarchies. That demoralizes any organization. Competent people leave, because you cannot be governed by someone who knows less than you, who lacks probity and a sense of country. At once the phenomenon of corruption sets in, taking advantage of the disintegration of the institutions.

**Let us think a little about what you have just said. We cannot be governed by people who “know less than you”. And what has happened to us all this time is that we have been governed by people who know less than a large part of the country’s population. Why have we Venezuelans allowed this?**

Yes, that is a painful question. Chávez was not interested in government as such. He was interested in his political project, in being in the spotlight. He was interested in changing the flag, the coat of arms; in changing the name of the country, leaving a different mark, his own projection. Power.

You ask: why have we allowed that? To answer, one has to open another chapter. What happened is that we spent ten years destroying the substance of our democracy. In the media we lived through a relentless criticism of everything that was done, said or proposed. The rise of anti-politics.

**But that is also the role of the media.**

The role of the media certainly includes criticism. But there was an interest on their part in directing the life of the country.

**Don’t you think that this role of the media arose from the very ineffectiveness of those in government, of party leaders, with regard to the probity they should have shown?**

Every political form decays. Our democratic life became somewhat oligarchic: the phenomenon called “cogollocracia”, rule by party cliques. Then the possibility arises of thinking that one can govern from the media. Then we went back to the chapter of the saviour: what is needed here is a total change, and for that a military man. We chose a military man, doubling as a demagogue, who was not prepared to govern.

A process begins of destroying the country’s institutions, of Venezuelans confronting one another. Perhaps because, when the moment came, there were not enough voices to oppose what was happening. Yet this truly lamentable and painful stage in the country’s history is still continuous with our long succession of military governments.

What was the different stage? The democratic Civil Republic, which lasted from 1958 to 1999.

When one considers it, it seems that the right way to put it is that it was a *historical singularity*. It had no precedent, and nothing like it has followed. How was it possible? It is something to meditate on, above all for young people who want to devote themselves to politics.

The causes of any social process are many, and various things could be argued, from international influence to oil, whatever you like. For me what was crucial (the expression may be a little clumsy, imprecise) was that the civilian caudillos submitted to the law. If one says “caudillos” it seems the expression is not correct; but what do I mean by that?

The people who led the political movements, and made democracy possible, were respected people with followers. They had popular support, a following not imposed by force of arms or by persecution. And those figures put the law first. With different styles, but with the concrete decision to uphold it. They shared the project of establishing democracy and achieving the country’s development. That is where one has to begin judging the problem. I give great importance to the two founding figures, Betancourt and Caldera, without a doubt. That is why I find Gehard Cartay’s book *Caldera y Betancourt. Constructores de la democracia* so valuable.

So we had the Civil Republic, which broke out of the negative cycle of that history of ruptures and new beginnings. We had democratic elections, the handing over of power, the transfer of command. With all the imperfections proper to human beings and to a condition like ours, in which there was no settled democratic tradition. That is why I think we must insist on considering how it was possible.

**Let us think about the young: with this deconstruction the country has undergone, in which there has been an attempt to erase historical memory, what can young people learn from all this?**

We must not forget that there is also the tradition of what Mijares called *lo afirmativo venezolano*, the affirmative in Venezuela. It translates, first of all, into an aspiration to an orderly civil life, not to government by force. That aspiration has not been erased; it is present. The great teacher Rómulo Gallegos, for example, is an eminently civilian figure.

**Venezuela was an example of democratic development for all the peoples of the world. That ended with Chávez. Back then we knew how to live together; we knew the rules of the democratic game. Now, at this moment of reconstruction, is it possible, in the Venezuelan soul, in what was part of our traditions, to recover that way of living together?**

At least we have the aspiration.

**With chavismo, how are we going to rebuild concord in the Venezuelan soul? That relative of mine whom I said all sorts of things to: how am I going to ask for forgiveness? All this goes beyond rebuilding institutions; it is about rebuilding the heart, which was broken.**

Which was broken, exactly. Hearts must be rebuilt. We need people who can convey a message. Voices from the Church, from the academy, from politics, who can speak in a positive sense to help rebuild hearts. That has a precondition: the word has to be true. To be real, love must be grounded in recognizing the other person and in the desire to promote their good. That presupposes rational discernment; it is not something purely emotional.

But our future at this moment is very uncertain.

**That is what I was going to say: reconstruction is very uncertain. But the key could be in those young Venezuelans who are here and whom teachers like you have to educate. We are also talking about the young as the key.**

We need young people who are truly educated, not with ideological brushstrokes but with a real understanding of the problems and love for the country.

This is where the problem of the colonial mentality comes in. Our attitude is to think that what is good always comes from abroad; that people have to prove themselves abroad; in short, that what we have here is not good. We have to live on imports: imported laws, systems, architectural forms, fashions in dress. But how can a country be built that way? When the point of reference keeps changing, the continuity needed to achieve anything is lacking. There are national products one could develop and improve: in architecture, certainly, in food, in fashion and in art. To reach the international level. But that presupposes continuity and a valuing of what we do here.

**The disarticulation has also caused a break with the traditionally known lines of power and brings insecurity to the country. And so some use that to justify “the gringos coming in”: that we Venezuelans were not going to be capable on our own of governing, or of putting together again a system that brings us some benefit.**

One hears the argument. One understands it a little, but one cannot help thinking that it is a new manifestation of the Venezuelan’s ancestral pessimism.

For the first time in its history as an independent nation, Venezuela has a de facto government imposed by a foreign power. I mentioned, with Briceño Iragorry, the dialectic of presumption and pessimism from which we suffer. We believe things are easy to achieve. When we see that it does not work, we say that nothing can be done here. We need not only a saviour, but now an external saviour, because we cannot cope with ourselves. That is not acceptable. From the truth of the Venezuelan tradition, from the truth of what we have done in our history, despite everything, it is not acceptable.

It is true that the country is in the worst condition we have lived through in a very long time: because of the disarticulation, the corruption that came with it, the squandering, the failure of the electrical, road and water infrastructure and of public services. That is evident. Moreover, it caused such an exodus that the Venezuelan diaspora is unbelievable for a country that has not suffered a war. We stopped having a common project; oppression began and, with it, an “every man for himself”. A large part of the people best prepared to carry the weight of the country left. With that, it might seem that we are definitely beyond remedy.

But the country’s condition cannot be remedied from outside, still less by people who, judging by their record, are after Venezuela’s resources. There is no solution to be had from a foreign government.

**With all that strength gone from the country, how can those who remain here be taught to think, to learn everything we have talked about?**

That is the great challenge, obviously, and it is no small one. In Venezuela the educational problem is crucial. The people who were capable of making the Civil Republic had the chance to be educated, to acquire mental discipline, to write and to think, to have a sustained purpose.

**In our character, what must we correct so as not to fail again and to enter the twenty-first century for good? Because perhaps we are not yet in the twenty-first century…**

That question is very important. There is an essential virtue we must cultivate in Venezuela: constancy. What does constancy rest on? On a sense of duty. What I am doing I have to do because it falls to me. It is valuable, good. I do not let myself be carried away by whatever distracts me, because I have a purpose to which I am committed. And at bottom, sustaining everything, is love for Venezuela. If you do not love the country, you will not be able to have a purpose and carry it through with constancy.

Without going very far, there are cases in the recent past showing that it is not true that we are incapable of anything good. One is the System of Youth Orchestras. Something so marvellous that it has drawn attention all over the world. It was done here. The inconstant Venezuelans in serious training to acquire a musical quality valid anywhere. We are not a cursed people, condemned to failure. That is not true. To have promoted the System, as José Antonio Abreu did, and he deserves full recognition for it, is impressive. The good is possible. It has to be said.

**What do you like most about being Venezuelan?**

There are many pleasant things about the Venezuelan character. Certainly egalitarianism, one of our great achievements, with its plainness in dealing with others.

The Venezuelan has an extraordinary capacity for linguistic innovation. That capacity is a talent that enriches life, literature and everyday conversation.

He has a very valuable capacity for enterprise, for initiative, for wit. We are impulsive, as we have said. But with that, we are capable of enormous generosity.

And we have spark.

**That spark has allowed us to survive pain and keep hope alive. Where do you keep your hope for the rebuilding of Venezuela? How do you live it?**

Hope is a theological virtue. We believers hope in God, in God’s help, and in the possibility of reaching God precisely through his help.

From a human point of view, we know that action is more important than passivity. By acting, people develop. One acts to achieve the good. Evil is a failure. If you project the light of theological hope onto everyday human reality, you see that rebuilding is worthwhile because it is good in itself and fulfils what God wants. That is the foundation of hope in the struggle.

**I am going to do what kids do on TikTok: give me two keys for rebuilding the country.**

One is *probity*, which must translate into seeking the common good. Serving the common good, not the interest of a faction, still less of a single person. That is key. It is one of the first principles. Without that reference we cannot build the country.

Everyone knows that life is a process and requires effort so that disordered tendencies do not prevail. That is why the struggle cannot be set aside. So *constancy* again becomes an important virtue, because things are never done once and for all.

**Do you think it has been worth devoting yourself to the trench of thought instead of choosing politics?**

In the case of devoting oneself to philosophy, everything starts with questions that present themselves to you. You begin to see that you need answers, and you arrive at philosophy. If you go in search of answers and try to do it as well as possible, it will have been worthwhile. Questions and reflection bring you before the mystery of what is real and to the conviction that, like Socrates, we know nothing. Only God is wise. Man searches. Whether you have been able to contribute something or not, that cannot be calculated.

**Are you writing anything in particular at the moment? A new book?**

Pope Leo XIV has spoken, in *Magnifica humanitas*, of building a city centred on the common good. Which, he says, “demands above all building on the rock of our relationship with God”. That is clear in the Christian faith, but it must also be clear in our human understanding of the reality of the person. He goes on: “To build in the good means accepting the limits and fragility of humanity without considering them an error to be corrected”. Those are two tremendous problems in contemporary thought: the denial of human nature in the attempt to overcome it, and forgetting that “in Him we live and move and have our being”, as Saint Paul said at the Areopagus. Without that, a society in the good cannot be built, as the Pope reminds us. That interests me very much from an anthropological point of view. I am working to understand it better.
