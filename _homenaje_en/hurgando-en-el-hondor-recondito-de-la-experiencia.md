## A philosophy of his own

What is deepest and most heartfelt in the philosophy of Rafael Tomás Caldera Pietri lies at that border where philosophy becomes meditation and meditation becomes philosophy. Where philosophy does not thereby lose rigour; and who knows whether it is there that it truly attains it.

One might think that this joining comes about through a chance affinity between the philosopher’s character and the nature of what he studies. In this case, however, the conjunction seems to arise also from an intrinsic demand of what is studied: the metaphysical contemplation of spiritual experience, in its cognitive, affective and practical dimensions.

If the philosopher’s temperament has played a part in his philosophy, this shows not only in the meditative pace of his approaches but also in his choice of subjects.

This attention to the most radical metaphysical questions, and in the most radical way, gradually creates something like complicity between reader and writer. Perhaps it is his art of disappearing, and his delicate respect for whoever reads him, that does most to create that “climate”.

That is how Rafael Tomás Caldera manages, before long, to bring us into his own reading. Reading him, one is invited to read, and to read better, Plato, Aristotle, Augustine, Thomas Aquinas, Maritain, Gilson and Geiger among the philosophers; but also Gallegos, Bello, Eliot, Chesterton, Saint-Exupéry, Tolkien, Lewis, Newman, Frankl and so many others. Caldera knows how to find in their writings brief passages that are true “needles in a haystack”, whose philosophical potential may have gone unnoticed even by the authors themselves.

## A journey to the depths of spiritual experience

This disposition of soul that Caldera reveals, both in choosing his subjects and in treating them, can be seen from his earliest metaphysical writings. His doctoral thesis at Fribourg, Switzerland, on judgement by inclination, supervised by the learned Dominican L. B. Geiger, shows clearly how rigorous research can be combined with close attention to the content of an affective spiritual experience. This contemplative inquiry, directed at the heart of experience, would grow stronger over the years, particularly in works such as *La primera captación intelectual* (The First Intellectual Apprehension), *Entender es decir* (To Understand Is to Say), *Misterio de lo real. Vocación al amor* (The Mystery of the Real. A Vocation to Love), *Nostalgia y trascendencia* (Nostalgia and Transcendence) and *En busca de Aquel que Es* (In Search of Him Who Is).

*La primera captación intelectual* deals no longer with a predominantly affective spiritual experience but with the most radical of all possible intellectual experiences. In this landmark work Caldera treats the first affirmation of reality, the affirmation of the actuality of what exists: an affirmation that arises in every human being from the most hidden cognitive levels of the soul. This apprehension shows itself from earliest childhood and, with growing brilliance, never ceases to illuminate the life of the intellect. A mysterious apprehension “at the heart of the real”, preconscious at first, but one to which all the other apprehensions of the mind end up being led back, with a temporal or existential priority, and not only an analytical one.

In *Entender es decir* our author recovers and explains the metaphysical grasp of the relations between word and thought. To do so he explores the preconscious roots of the mind, the emergence of that intimate “marriage” between word and concept (to put it in Gabriela Mistral’s words). He also ventures here into a metaphysical phenomenology of the way we humans shape our own identity.

In *Nostalgia y trascendencia* and *En busca de Aquel que Es*, Caldera’s metaphysical phenomenology reaches heights of sublime subtlety in exploring the human heart: both in the search for self-knowledge and in the discovery of what we have always longed for, the greatness and immensity of “Him Who Is”.

## Reading Augustine in the manner of Thomas, and Thomas in the manner of Augustine

It is not unthinkable that, just as Thomas Aquinas read Augustine of Hippo nine centuries later and brought to light potentialities that Augustine did not actualize, so we too may perhaps today, seven centuries later, read Thomas Aquinas and actualize potentialities he did not suspect, or that his life was too short to develop. More surprising still is that a group of important scholars of Aquinas have carried out what I venture to call “an Augustinian reading of Thomas”, or better, “a Thomism in the spirit of Augustine”.

The names that come especially to mind are Aimé Forest, Joseph Rassam, Pierre Fontan and André Marc. And there will surely be others, each in his own way and his own style. To this company we must undoubtedly add, and not as one of the least, Rafael Tomás Caldera.

This author’s works, as he himself recommends of others, are to be read and reread. Understanding him well requires immersing oneself “in his music”, and that does not always come at first. The brevity of his books is, in this sense, highly deceptive. His simple, clear, elegant manner conceals more richness than appears at first sight, and often obliges us, if we are to understand him fully, to go back to the sources to which he himself refers us.

Gilson used to say that Thomism, at first, confronts the beginner with an aridity that can be discouraging. But, he went on, once one is used to it, there begins to be no other atmosphere in which one can breathe. These words surely apply as well to this distinguished disciple of Thomas, who does honour to Venezuela.
