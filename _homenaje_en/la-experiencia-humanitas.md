Rafael Tomás Caldera Pietri is one of the central figures of contemporary Venezuelan thought. A philosopher, scholar and essayist, his work covers, with remarkable depth and rigor, a range of fields: metaphysics, philosophical anthropology, epistemology, ethics, politics, education and culture.

One of the distinctive notes of his intellectual work is its pedagogical character. Caldera does not hoard for himself the truths he manages to reach, but seeks to make them available to everyone.

For Caldera this task, beyond being an act of generosity, is a duty of justice, since (as he argues in *El oficio del sabio*, The Calling of the Wise Man) the great human goods, truth among them, “are possessed only in communion.” Hence he assigns to the thinker the task of keeping visible to citizens the truth about human fulfillment and the demands of a just common life.

Hand in hand with that civic commitment, Caldera has developed throughout his life a side of his work that is little visible to the general public and yet of great consequence for the destiny of the Republic: a sustained effort to contribute to the political formation of new generations of young people with a vocation for politics and public service.

## The political formation of the next generation

Caldera holds that the social order is not static: each generation must take up anew the problems of living together and find answers suited to its own time.

From that conviction comes one of his main concerns about the country’s future: preparing the next generation, in particular those young people who, as he writes in his introduction to *El poder y la justicia* (Power and Justice), “feel called to devote their best efforts to building a better, more just and more fraternal society.”

It falls to the ruler (to the politician) to discern what is best for all. However, Caldera warns in his essay “Formación para la política” (Formation for Politics), that delicate task “requires a certain quality of person: not just anyone could do it.” No one is born “fit to govern”; that fitness is the fruit of an acquired maturity, the maturity of someone who has cultivated the qualities needed to see, judge and act for the common good.

Caldera has devoted valuable efforts to helping cultivate that maturity in young people with a political vocation: seminars, lectures, study circles, personal mentoring and a fruitful body of writing in which he develops the foundations of that task. Humanitas was one of them.

## The Humanitas experience

In 1985, under the drive and leadership of Fernando Egaña, a group of young people who shared a vocation for politics founded Humanitas, a civil association whose purpose was to promote the political formation of its members and young people’s critical reflection on the country’s problems.

The engine of that initiative was a study circle led by Rafael Tomás Caldera. That activity gave meaning and quality to the association’s other projects.

## Reading rigorously, thinking clearly

For three years, some fifteen of us met once a week, for two and a half hours, to read, analyze and discuss a set of texts chosen by Caldera.

Before each session, all of us had to have read the assigned chapter or section. The meeting began with a brief summary and an opening reflection by one of the participants; then the passages Caldera indicated were read aloud. At each passage the professor commented, asked questions and led us to pin down the meaning of key terms or the logical relationship between the different parts of the argument.

Around this method a dialogue arose that forced our minds to go out and meet the text. Caldera asked and asked again, and made us be precise so as to avoid any hasty conjecture. The aim of that unhurried reading and rigorous analysis was to discipline our minds, to teach us to inquire with intellectual honesty into what the author really meant to communicate.

## Acquiring principles

Over those years we studied four texts. We began with Andrés Bello’s address on *La misión de la universidad* (The Mission of the University), which warns that a republic needs citizens able to think for themselves and that this ability is formed, not improvised. We went on to the pastoral constitution *Gaudium et spes*, which places the human person as the principle, subject and end of social institutions. We continued with *Reflexiones de La Rábida* (Reflections from La Rábida), by former president Rafael Caldera, which explores Latin America through the relationship between social science and political action; and we finished with *La especificidad de la democracia cristiana* (The Specific Character of Christian Democracy), by the same author, which examines democracy as a way of life.

Under Caldera’s guidance, those readings allowed us to go deeper into the nature of man, of society, of power and of justice. From them we drew a set of principles, of coordinates for judgment and action: among others, the primacy of the common good as the raison d’être of the political community; the dignity of the human person as the moral limit of every collective project; the understanding of power as a means of action and not an end in itself; and the requirement that political action remain subordinate to ethical criteria.

## Cultivating prudence

In light of those notions and principles, Caldera would put before us concrete problems that a politician faces. In those discussions he encouraged us to consider the circumstances, to weigh the different alternatives and possible contingencies, and to defend with reasons the solution that seemed right to us.

That intellectual effort of relating principles to concrete circumstances helped cultivate in us the virtue the Greeks called *phronesis* and the Romans *prudentia*: the practical wisdom that makes it possible to discern what should be done in each concrete situation. The exercise also made us see that governing requires technical competence as well.

## Firmness and courage to carry out what has been planned

Through the example of historical figures and episodes, Caldera showed us how, in difficult circumstances, it was the leader’s character that made the difference: fortitude, to stand by costly or unpopular decisions; temperance, to master impulses before the temptations of power; perseverance, to hold course towards long-term goals; and, finally, the will to justice and the readiness to share morally in the life and burdens of the people one serves.

Despite the difficult circumstances Venezuela is going through, Caldera remains hopeful in his task of contributing to the political formation of the next generation. In his own words: “Venezuela awaits a new generation. The generation that will have to rebuild the country. A generation ready for sacrifice. A generation with judgment. A generation of character, committed to bringing about justice.”
