In his book *La existencia abierta: para lectores de El Principito* (The Open Existence: For Readers of *The Little Prince*), Rafael Tomás Caldera remarks that he has found Saint-Exupéry’s book to have “pages always fresh, delightful, full of suggestions” (p. 8). When I arrived at Universidad Simón Bolívar (USB) in 1998 to begin my first job as a professor of Philosophy (and the first woman in the department), my nerves and anxieties were calmed when I met a great teacher with a true vocation for teaching. Like the pages of *The Little Prince*, conversations with my esteemed friend and colleague always refreshed my spirit, gave me great pleasure and let me leave with new hope and new ideas about how to connect with my students at USB. Although almost three decades have passed since my first days in Caracas (I arrived in December 1997, in a simpler time), the picture of that period remains very clear in my mind and in my heart, where, as we learn in *The Little Prince*, what is essential is perceived. In moments when one feels alone, a strong friendship is needed, and that is exactly what I found in Rafael Tomás Caldera: a gifted philosopher and a human being full of virtues.

## No one regrets returning to contemplate what they admire and love

“No one regrets returning to contemplate what they admire and love” (*La existencia abierta*, p. 9). It is a great pleasure to remember my time at USB and my good fortune in having had the chance to philosophize with Rafael Tomás Caldera. From the beginning I was lucky to have a cultured, generous colleague, open to diverse philosophical styles. The philosophy department had an open existence, shaped in large part by Professor Caldera’s dedication. Through his work and his example he changed the lives of his students and colleagues: from his analysis of Thomas Aquinas on the first intellectual apprehension to his reflections on human worth and the essential role of literature in understanding the Venezuelan situation in its fullness. Walking in dialogue with Prof. Caldera, or reading him, one learns to reach a broader understanding of the world and of the human condition. When I moved from Caracas to Chicago, I took with me a new appreciation of the importance of contemplating North America and Latin America “as a whole” (*En busca de nuestra expresión*, p. 9). R. T. Caldera’s emphasis on harmony, honesty, appreciation of persons and shared values leads us towards a human expression.

## Literature and democracy in Venezuela

During my stay, in dialogue with R. T. Caldera and over the years of our friendship and of his works’ influence on me, I learned that knowing Venezuela required knowing Rómulo Gallegos, and vice versa, because “his literature is braided together with the country’s development: his writing (we may say) is inscribed, as an element and as a causal factor, in the coming into being of [Venezuelan] democracy in the life of the republic” (pp. 39-40). In this observation (from his essay “La emoción original de la bondad”, The Original Emotion of Goodness), Caldera invokes our dear colleague Luis Castro Leiva and the address he gave before the Congress of the Republic on the 40th anniversary of the overthrow of the last dictatorship (23 January 1958). The enduring literature of Venezuela, the works of Teresa de la Parra, Mariano Picón Salas and Arturo Uslar Pietri, opens the way to the joy of the homeland, to good reading and to the human condition that leads us to the universal and the beautiful.

This does not mean we are blind to “the pain of the homeland,” a recurring expression in the work of Rómulo Gallegos, as Caldera notes in “La emoción original de la bondad” (part of the collection *En busca de nuestra expresión*, In Search of Our Expression). Desolation, R. T. Caldera explains in his essay, is born of ignorance, of “lack of cultivation” (p. 43). In *Nuevo Mundo y mentalidad colonial* (New World and Colonial Mentality), R. T. Caldera stresses education as formation (in Germany there is a whole tradition of speaking of *Bildung*, the cultivation of the spirit), as opposed to the colonial mentality. The well-cultivated person is one who knows how “to make values their own, to embody them in their attitudes and to carry out works (actions and productions) of good quality” (*Mentalidad colonial*, p. 91). Educating in Venezuela becomes *celebrating Venezuela* when we speak of a figure like Professor R. T. Caldera, devoted to cultivating the values of a strong and good democracy: a democracy that forms its citizens and opens the way to a deep understanding of the human condition.

## Landscapes

When I left USB, leaving the community that had welcomed me with hospitality and love, I felt the sadness of saying goodbye not only to dear friends but also to the dazzling landscape created by the Ávila. At that moment R. T. Caldera gave me a book that has allowed me to carry the landscape with me for decades: a work on Pedro Ángel González, painter of the Ávila, through which I keep access to what is essential in the Venezuelan landscape. I left Venezuela with a precious memory but, above all, with a friendship that gives me light, beauty and lasting hope, as well as a model of how to become a great teacher of philosophy. I can say without exaggeration that I had the chance to learn from a true master, a *super teacher*, someone always open to students and devoted to teaching, to sharing ideas and time. As in the minds of his students, Prof. R. T. Caldera left “a constant, pleasant mark” (*La existencia abierta*, p. 62) on my memory and in my heart.
