There are authors who speak of what it means to be Venezuelan. Faced with that question, I might mention Teresa de la Parra, and Rafael Tomás Caldera might mention Mario Briceño Iragorry. Without a doubt we would all refer to Rómulo Gallegos. That literature, in them and in many other authors, should have built an answer to the surprisingly still current question *who are we?* is no trivial matter.

“The question” of our identity resonates with us naturally, because it seems to be characteristic of Latin Americans to debate again and again, in the cultural sphere, the absence of such a definition, the search for the place where it might be found, and the declaration that the solution to that problem is unattainable.

It is a problem that Carlos Rangel tried in some way to answer in his well-known book *Del buen salvaje al buen revolucionario* (published in English as *The Latin Americans: Their Love-Hate Relationship with the United States*), whose reception (whatever one makes of its arguments) shows with forceful clarity how the longing to define what we call “our identity” never loses its relevance.

In 1980 Rafael Tomás Caldera published *La respuesta de Gallegos* (Gallegos’s Answer): three essays that very intelligently address not only Latin Americans’ long-standing concern with their own identity, but also the way the questions have been framed and the missteps in the search for answers.

Hence the marvel of what Caldera promises: “to gather, in examining the problems, the contribution of Gallegos and, in particular, to determine the germinal intuition of his work.” It is a warm and intense conversation with an interlocutor of stature, in order to think through a problem that concerns us all. Gallegos thus becomes a companion in inquiry rather than an object of study.

## “Pataruco”: art as the embodiment of an answer

The first of the essays starts from an uncomfortable premise: Venezuelans (Latin Americans in general) have spent decades asking ourselves who we are, without ever arriving at an answer that leaves us at peace. Caldera gathers the diagnoses of Carlos Rangel, Juan Liscano and Arturo Uslar Pietri, and notes something the hasty reader might overlook: the insistence of the question is, in itself, a more revealing symptom than any answer we might give it.

It seems that it is not so much that we are ignorant of our origin, our roots, our history, but that we are searching for the meaning of an existence that has too often been understood as failure.

The distinction between the point of view of the one who creates (the artist) and that of the one who interprets (the critic) is a starting point for understanding something of the cultural anguish that besets us. For we expect the artist to work for the critics, demanding of them a program of “Latin American authenticity” instead of letting the work be born of an inner necessity. This is a key point, because originality is free when it is not being sought.

This is clearly seen in the episode told in Gallegos’s story, when a piece of music is produced without “being sought” and something absolutely original emerges that speaks for itself, and more of itself, than any other construction that criticism could produce.

## Between pessimism and utopia

The second essay, “Pesimismo y presunción” (Pessimism and Presumption), offers a vivid understanding of a polarization that seems recurrent in our character: the swing from resigned hopelessness to boundless messianism. In Gallegos, and particularly in *Reinaldo Solar*, with the hero’s departure for Europe in search of a destiny he cannot find at home, Caldera finds the fictional expression of a peculiar, almost paradoxical patriotism: one that shows itself, as Gallegos himself puts it through one of his characters, more in renunciation and farewell than in staying and in daily work. It is a negative patriotism, the text says, that loves the adventure of the grand gesture because it is incapable of sustaining the small effort of every day.

With *Reinaldo Solar*, Caldera confirms the commonplace of “the Venezuelan who leaves,” and beyond that he opens the door to the question of hope as a properly cultural problem: where does a hope come from that can only express itself as flight or as utopia, and what would it take for it to express itself, instead, as perseverance?

The question, written more than four decades ago, has lost none of its relevance for a country still torn between despair and the illusion of total solutions, while it reads its present in the polarized terms of *leaving* or *staying*.

## The struggle between civilization and barbarism, revisited

The third essay, “Apelación a la raza” (Appeal to the Race), takes up the most widely named and discussed theme in our literature: the struggle between civilization and barbarism, which runs from Bello and Sarmiento to Gallegos and beyond. With a methodological lucidity that I personally find exhilarating throughout the book, Caldera warns that every *commonplace* carries a risk: that of becoming a substitute for thinking instead of its starting point.

Examining a little-known lecture by Gallegos, “Las tierras de Dios” (The Lands of God), he distinguishes three levels of that age-old struggle (man’s mastery over nature, over his own passions and over social violence) to show that the civilization-barbarism opposition is not so much a geographical or racial formula as a drama played out, above all, within each person.

## Gallegos as a master

The brilliance of this book, which I have tried to show in so brief a space, lies not in Gallegos and the depth of his work, but in the intellectual attitude on which this dialogue rests. Here is a philosopher who approaches literature in order to exchange questions and find answers. Caldera treats Gallegos as one treats a genuine master: listening to him, understanding him and meshing his ideas with our own questions. That is why the book ages so well: it does not offer closed answers about “what is Venezuelan,” but a method (patient, attentive to detail) for continuing to ask honestly.

To reread *La respuesta de Gallegos* today is to confirm that the best way to honor a master, whether Gallegos for Caldera or Caldera for so many of his disciples, is not to repeat their conclusions but to inherit their way of asking. For me this is one of the most valuable legacies of these pages, which are not intent on reaching final answers, but on dwelling rigorously on the meaning of each question and on the value of each small approach to its reason for being.
