In the 1980s and 1990s professional philosophy was taking hold in Venezuela, and it was written horribly. Texts were a display of gibberish to please evaluators and initiates. The less they were understood, the better. It was clear that the new philosophers had not the slightest intention of taking wisdom to the agora. Against the current, I had become fascinated with the medievals, from Augustine to Thomas Aquinas. Their pre-Cartesian realism, which begins with things and not with the consciousness that thinks them, relieved me of modern navel-gazing.

That is how I came to Rafael Tomás Caldera, who did not teach at my university but at the Simón Bolívar. I first saw him at a symposium at Celarg. Dr. Caldera was speaking about the Angelic Doctor. Of the former I knew nothing then beyond the prejudices of the time, when being progressive was still the fashion. That day I heard him read and comment on a text with a clarity and a grace unheard of in the trade. My conclusion was immediate: if I ever write philosophy, I want it to sound like this. I went out to look for his books, and that is how I fell for the one I review here, *De la lectura. Del arte de escribir* (On Reading. On the Art of Writing), which I have used ever since, even to earn my living in Madrid. I read it in an afternoon and I am still learning it.

The author presents those seventy-seven pages, in two parts, one on reading and one on writing, as “some brief notes”, a sketch drawn “as if with a dark pencil”, and he opens the second part by recalling that the art can be learned but not taught. That modesty governs the whole book and determines its ending.

It struck me that anyone should think it necessary to remind university students how to read. The reason lies in one sentence: “Reading is the operation correlative to writing.” Writing composes a meaningful whole out of words; reading rebuilds that whole from the words. That is why it is in synthesis that “the reader’s craft comes closest to the writer’s.” Anyone who has corrected a badly thought-out manuscript recognizes this, because whoever synthesizes badly is left with spare parts, like the inexperienced mechanic who took the engine apart and cannot put it back together. Whoever reads badly writes badly and does not even notice, because they lack the eye with which one reviews one’s own work. The book also asks for a virtue: to approach the text without the noise we bring in from the street, because “the book, like the sensitive plant, closes its leaves at an impertinent touch.”

There is one passage I have disagreed with ever since, or that I still do not understand. When dealing with criticism, Rafael Tomás admits that the appraisal of a text may lead to a recommendation “that it be restricted to the use of specialists or that, simply, it not circulate.” And this is no isolated slip, because the chapter that calls for openness advises stopping the reading of what is harmful, as one spits out spoiled food. It sounds to me like indexes of forbidden books and content warnings, which make me equally uncomfortable under one flag or the other. But then the lack of judgment with which things are published and read almost makes me agree with him.

If reading and writing are the same operation, the second half of the book is the first seen from the other side. It begins with what one does before writing, which is to set oneself a subject. That sounds obvious, and it is not. Many people write without having asked themselves what they want to say, or whether they have anything to say, and then complain of having lost a thread they never had. Writing meaningfully begins by marking out an aspect, a perspective, a way of treating a matter, and almost everything that gets stuck in a manuscript gets stuck there.

Everything else follows from the first rule. The book takes it from the prologue to *Don Quixote*, which advises writing “plainly, with meaningful words, honest and well placed.” “Let each word carry what it says,” wrote our greatest poet. Easily said. Four centuries of endorsement and Cervantes’s signature, and still, in the guild’s journals, writing plainly would pass for naivety. What follows are four ways of keeping the rule: keep to the subject, remove what is superfluous, put one thing after another, go straight ahead. None of the four is simple. “I want terrifying exactitudes,” Rafael Cadenas asks in the same poem.

And then there is the framework that does not show itself. The clarity of a piece of writing, he says, rests on unity, coherence and emphasis, and whoever observes them attains “the clarity of a small (or great) masterpiece.” Anyone who has read Aquinas recognizes the scaffolding, because integrity, due proportion and clarity are the three conditions of beauty in the *Summa*, here put to work under other names. The author does not cite them once, nor does he need to. A few pages earlier he has written, almost in passing, that adjusting the length of a paragraph “greatly helps the clarity, and therefore the beauty, of the exposition,” and that “therefore” quietly carries seven centuries of theory.

The deepest part comes at the end. Every writer has felt “the hard limit of language, which seems to resist expression,” when it is precisely what makes expression possible. What is essential remains ineffable, and it is enough for the words to put the reader in a position to glimpse it. The quality of what is written does not depend on formal aspects or “on observing some consecrated set of rules”; it depends on “the measure of the thing.” Some lines from *Martín Fierro* (“don’t tune the instrument / just for the pleasure of talking”) precede the final recommendation: to write only if we have something to say, and otherwise to keep silent, because that silence gives value to the word. There the booklet of advice becomes a poetics in the ancient sense. Manuals of style teach how to write, and even how to write successfully; these “brief notes” ask us to consider whether we have to.

Ten printings in thirty-eight years, from Ediciones Dimensiones to Vadell Hermanos and from there to Rialp, plus the one we prepared at El Estilete that remained in proofs. And piles of photocopies and files that students and graduates have been passing around for years, without anyone’s permission. A book whose author warns that he has sought no originality, because in matters of advice everything must be backed by a tradition, can hardly complain about circulating without an owner.
