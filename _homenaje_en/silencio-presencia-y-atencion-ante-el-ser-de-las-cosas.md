## What do we know first?

Let us picture ourselves in a room: there we find a cup of coffee, an armchair and a book. We can identify these objects according to what each one is. Each has its own way of being and its own function.

If we ask what we know first about that cup, that armchair or that book, we would probably say the strong smell of the coffee, the colour of the cup, the shape of the armchair or the size of the book. Perhaps, going a little deeper, we would say that what we know first about the coffee is that we recognize it as coffee and not tea, or that we can tell that the armchair is not a bed but an armchair. In other words, we might say that when we are before an object, a being, what we know first is its essence, and even the physical features that follow from it.

However (and this is the thesis Rafael Tomás Caldera offers us in *La primera captación intelectual*), what we know first of the coffee when we drink it, of the book on the desk, or even of a person standing before us, is not their essence. What we know first, what first “touches” our intellect, what it makes contact with and is primarily directed to, is their being: “what is apprehended, whatever it may be, can be symbolized by the exclamation ‘it is!’ (…). We stand before the beginning of thought: the act of consciousness” (*La primera captación intelectual*, p. 19).

## What is known first: being

*Being* is what the intellect conceives first. But what does it mean that *being* is what is known *first*? (cf. p. 9).

The word “first” does not refer to a beginning in time. We are not speaking of a succession of acts of knowledge in which the first step would be to grasp the being of a thing. When we say “first known” we mean a cognitive priority (cf. p. 17): we cannot think anything without thinking it as something *that is*. The apprehension of *being* is the condition that makes every other kind of knowledge and experience possible.

When I know something (a cup, for example), in one and the same act I grasp a being: that the cup is “something” (its essence or way of being) and “that it is” (the fact that it exists). But what sustains the knowledge of that cup’s essence is its being. That “this cup is” is what remains continuously present in my intellect, like a light. Thanks to that light I can recognize the cup as a cup; that is, I can know the essence of something. That is why, in saying that what we know first are beings (and that we know them thanks to their act of being), we affirm that “first” does not refer to a succession of acts of knowledge but to a timelessness that occurs when one abides in being. In this immediate apprehension time does not run: one contemplates.

## So obvious that it transforms us without our noticing

It is worth pointing out how obvious this knowledge is. Without realizing it, we are genuinely open to being, to reality. It is an openness that characterizes our human condition. We might say that the human being is the one inclined to grasp the being that gives existence to every reality. This is the invitation of Rafael Tomás Caldera (and, at bottom, of all metaphysics): to think the obvious.

There is a correlation between the being of what is real and our intellect, a relation that works without our consciously intervening: it happens to us without our noticing. Everything we understand, we understand in relation to being; without it we could not think, say or express anything.

Realizing that things are, and that being acts as a light that precedes us, allows us to understand our human condition better. The human being is the one who is awakened by the being of things and who lives in a certain tension towards it: “the subject, we might say, thirsts to understand” (p. 61).

We could say that what is most characteristic of the human being is the capacity for contemplation: the act of understanding and, with it, the act of contemplating being. For when we understand the being of a tree, we are not analysing the tree: we are simply enjoying it.

And that is where attention and presence before the being of things appear. Things simply are; they need neither our knowledge nor our intervention in order to be. We cannot control being: we can only receive it, pay attention to it, and remain passive, though awake, in its presence. Simone Weil herself (*Gravity and Grace*) deepens this stance when she holds that “attention consists in suspending our thought, leaving it detached, empty and ready to be penetrated by the object” (section “Attention and Will”). The act of knowing is stripped of any trace of capture or intellectual possession.

The discursive, rational function of our intelligence must not stifle our receptive and contemplative dimension, an essential feature of our way of knowing (cf. *La primera captación intelectual*, pp. 53-54). It is the immediate apprehension of being, which comes as a gift. Being as such admits of no control: it simply offers itself to us as light. Might it be that human growth consists in welcoming what is given rather than trying to dominate it? Is it not there that the possibility of a deeper authenticity opens up?

## The essential becomes visible in the light of being

To conclude, let us take another of Rafael Tomás Caldera’s writings: *La existencia abierta* (The Open Existence), his commentary on *The Little Prince*. From it we choose three sentences that illuminate with extraordinary simplicity the thesis that being is what is known first, and invite us to silent meditation:

a) “Time given (lost is a manner of speaking) is made of attention” (*La existencia abierta*, p. 23).

b) “There is no meaningful form or figure capable of capturing our attention, of distracting us from what reveals itself with force: the very presence of what is” (p. 26).

c) “Being shines, radiates. Now we perceive it, and we realize that it has always been there, beneath every figure, in every concrete form we encounter. The reality of the real, which we are unable to reduce to the scope of our words” (p. 26).

These sentences offer us a compass for life: the human gaze must remain attentive before the light of the being of what is real and keep a deep silence. The voice of being is almost imperceptible. “What is essential is invisible to the eye,” said Antoine de Saint-Exupéry. Being is so obvious that we do not see it, yet without it we could see nothing. That is why it deserves all our attention and our gratitude.
