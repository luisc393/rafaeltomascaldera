One might say that looking and listening are natural, involuntary acts, since nobody should have to learn to do what their biological condition already allows them to do. With Rafael Tomás Caldera, however, we discover that behind an apparently obvious premise there may lie a much more complex reality. And in this he closely resembles his master, Saint Thomas Aquinas.

Caldera shows, both in his work and in his way of being, that looking and listening require learning, a certain training and, above all, a proper disposition. They are capacities that reach their fullness only when they are educated and exercised, because their foundation is not only physical. In some way, opening oneself to what one sees or hears also involves an activity of the spirit. Caldera has understood well, with Saint Augustine and Saint Thomas, that before building any reasoning or explanation, what is fitting is to let reality reach us.

## Knowing how to look

In the first chapter of *La existencia abierta* (The Open Existence), Rafael Tomás Caldera presents the child as an archetype. He says that, in some way, we should aspire to be like children: not as a return to a stage of naivety or innocence, but as a form of growth. To mature intellectually and spiritually means recovering a gaze capable of wonder, one that does not reduce reality to what is already known. So the child, or rather the one who is like a child, prepares himself to receive the meaning or the truth of things. And this attitude towards the world, if we dare to go a little further, can be related to the Thomist conception of prudence.

In this sense, when Saint Thomas sets out the integral parts of prudence in *S. Th.* II-II, q. 49, two of them prove illuminating for understanding the intellectual figure of Rafael Tomás Caldera: *docilitas* and *solertia*. The first is the disposition to let oneself be taught by those who have travelled the path of truth before us; the second, the capacity to grasp quickly what is essential in a new reality and to judge it rightly. Both seem to converge in a singular way in Caldera’s work. His thought is docile because it converses with the great masters of the tradition, especially Saint Augustine and Saint Thomas, to whom he turns not as authorities replacing his own judgement but as interlocutors able to educate the intellect. Yet that docility does not end in sterile repetition. On the contrary, it becomes the basis of a genuine shrewdness that lets him question the challenges of the present with lucidity; and so he is also able to philosophize about matters that concern us today, such as artificial intelligence or the rise of new technologies. Thus, like the child, he ends up being docile and shrewd at the same time.

## Knowing how to listen

*La primera captación intelectual* (The First Intellectual Apprehension) and *Entender es decir* (To Understand Is to Say) are works worth returning to whenever we wish to understand better the situation of the one who listens. In the first book the author tells us: “Here we have the initial datum, which must govern all later rational elaboration. Namely, the apprehension of something, whatever it may be, as other than me and, for that reason, present to me who am (now) also present to it” (p. 27). Caldera shows us that first moment of consciousness in which we grasp ourselves in relation to what surrounds us; he associates it with awakening because it is something initial and not reasoned. This presence must be the condition of possibility of listening, what precedes the whole process.

In *Entender es decir* Caldera takes a further step: before what is real, the desire to know it must appear. But before any analysis or discourse we may undertake, the author points out that there is something already given to us: “a pre-understanding of being, of the good.” Understanding therefore requires a particular kind of attention to what is beginning to become present in us, respecting “an unconscious germination” that will later reach a fuller formulation. So before reasoning, judging or acting, one must listen: “To hear the inner voice, silent (as befits what is implicit) yet with all the force of what is effective and true” (p. 33). Listening is thus placed at the very origin of all understanding.

## A philosophy of attention

The mark of Saint Thomas and Saint Augustine on Rafael Tomás Caldera should be sought not only in the theses he shares with them; it can also be seen in a certain way of doing philosophy. In almost all his writings he takes the thread of a question and follows it backwards, trying to discover where it is tied. As has been said, he takes nothing for granted or as fully known. Indeed, he pays constant attention to the very data of existence, and for that reason almost anything can become an object of his study or interest. So we find works as varied as *De la lectura. Del arte de escribir* (On Reading. On the Art of Writing), *El uso del tiempo* (The Use of Time), *Sobre la naturaleza del amor* (On the Nature of Love) or “Inteligencia artificial y Estado docente” (Artificial Intelligence and the Teaching State), among others. Like his masters, Caldera understands that being attentive means resting one’s gaze on things and keeping silent in order to hear what is not immediately revealed.
