A voice crying in the wilderness. The expression is used colloquially in its most obvious sense: a message, a messenger, an isolated voice proclaiming a truth amid surroundings that are deaf, apathetic or emptied of meaning.

The negative view of the desert in culture and literature comes from its biological hostility and its symbolic weight. When we think of a desert, we picture lack of water, extreme climate, absence of food, total solitude.

A few years ago I heard Fr. Arturo Peraza, S.J., say at a meeting about Venezuela that we had to realize and accept that the country was *crossing the desert*. The phrase echoed in my head: can one cross a desert without knowing it, and without the aptitude and the attitude for it? And perhaps more importantly: can one get out of the desert without knowing the route, without a destination and a guide to show the way?

To get out of the desert, out of deserts, one needs to have and to impose order, patience and time, a leadership that gives meaning and direction to the march; discipline, conviction and a shared destination. All of this is necessary. But also necessary is the brave, sensible and transformative proclamation of a truth or a hope that prepares the way for something great, even when it is uttered from apparent solitude or from the vastness of a hostile environment.

That, without any doubt, has been the role taken on by a philosopher and intellectual such as Rafael Tomás Caldera, in the midst of a public debate dominated by immediacy and by convenient rhetoric of the moment: to stand as a *vox clamantis in deserto*.

In a Venezuela with few voices devoted to unhurried and rigorous thought, Caldera’s reflective work is an indispensable exercise of lucidity and ethical demand; his work reminds us that the thinker’s true responsibility is not to join in the urgency of the moment, but to keep the discipline of calm judgment, to name realities precisely and to provide fundamental categories for understanding the underlying problems.

Rafael Tomás Caldera insists, again and again, that our country is not going through an ordinary crisis. We are not facing only a macroeconomic imbalance or a passing political situation; we face a substantive dismantling that reaches the very root of our republican and human condition. The widespread collapse of public services, the fragmentation of territorial authority, the ruin of the productive apparatus and the searing drama of mass emigration are the visible manifestations of a much deeper ethical and cultural breakdown.

Rafael Tomás Caldera’s diagnosis of the Venezuelan situation unravels the causes of the crisis with philosophical, humanist and Christian rigor. Drawing on the classical anthropological principle that the order of society reflects the soul and the virtues of its citizens, Caldera shows that institutional collapse is the projection of a collective ethical straying. Venezuelan history has swung between two traditions: the *caudillo* tradition, based on arbitrary will, force and personalism; and the civil tradition, guided by the rule of law, institutions and harmonious coexistence. “Democracy as a political form, in an authoritarian country with a *caudillo* tradition, was made possible by the quality of certain men,” Rafael Tomás Caldera tells us.

The experience of the Civil Republic showed that it was possible to subordinate force to reason and to build a stable order through fundamental agreements and the constitutional framework of 1961. In the course of history, however, a decisive fragility set in: the illusion that institutions and laws can sustain themselves without the constant practice of civic virtue.

It is that constant practice of civic virtues that Rafael Tomás Caldera, following the Aristotelian tradition, highlights as *concord*: a kind of civic (or political) friendship consisting in the agreement of citizens’ wills about common interests, about what is useful and what is just. Or rather, in Caldera’s own words, “a basic consensus that makes life in community possible, a life that favors the fulfillment of persons. In sum, the common good.”

It is at this point in Rafael Tomás Caldera’s thought that the “voice crying out” does so with the greatest firmness, determination and force.

Faced with the evident and deep rupture the country is living through, Caldera does not fold his arms, does not give up, does not fall silent, does not become indifferent or confine himself to lament. Quite the opposite. He turns and speaks (with guidance at once reproving and wise) to the young, to those young people who wish (and how badly they are needed!) to devote themselves to politics.

The purpose of politics, Caldera tells us, is the common good, and in a world as dynamic as ours, the common good as the aim of political action will mean that each and every person passes from less human conditions of life to more human ones (cf. *El poder y la justicia. Para jóvenes políticos*).

In line with the teaching of the Church’s Magisterium, Caldera reiterates that the political obstacles that prevent the development of peoples are not merely mechanical or bureaucratic, but moral failings turned into “structures of sin.” Understood in this way, politics ceases to be a mere calculation of power (perhaps an ambition) or technical pragmatism and recovers its character as a high vocation and civic function, requiring those who take it on to do so not as a utilitarian trade but as the free commitment to direct their talent to ethical service, human dignity and the common destiny of society.

Rafael Tomás Caldera is undoubtedly a voice crying in the wilderness. But, as the Scriptures clearly show us, “crying in the wilderness” is not a testimony of failure or of futile isolation, but a vocation that bears the most momentous fruits in the history of salvation.
